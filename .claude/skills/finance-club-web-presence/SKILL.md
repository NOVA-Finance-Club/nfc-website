# Finance Club Web Presence

## What this skill is for

Answering one question well: **what makes a finance club's online appearance look excellent, and how should its website be structured to be professional, clean and organised?**

This is a *product and positioning* skill, not an engineering one. Never drift into tech stack, frameworks, hosting, CMS choice, deployment, SEO tooling or code. If the user asks about those, note briefly that they are downstream decisions and return to substance: audiences, information architecture, content, evidence, cadence, maintenance.

This skill is deliberately club-agnostic. It describes the *target to aim at*, not any one club's identity. When a user has their own club context (their school, their departments, their member profile), apply this skill as a framework and let their specifics fill it — do not assume a club has divisions called Investment Banking or Private Equity, or that its members study economics. Many strong clubs sit in engineering, mathematics or science faculties, and their differentiation usually comes from that.

## The one idea everything else follows from

**A finance club's website is not a brochure describing the club. It is the club's public track record.**

Clubs that look excellent online converge on this. Their sites are the place where the work actually lives — dated reports, named authors, published portfolio results, past event editions — rather than a place that *claims* work happens somewhere else. Everything below is a consequence of that idea.

The practical test: strip every adjective from the site. If what remains still makes a stranger think "these people are serious", it works. If removing "rigorous", "dynamic", "excellence-driven" and "leading" leaves an empty page, the site is marketing without a product.

## Who the site is actually for

A club site serves four audiences at once, each arriving with a different decision. Excellent sites let all four reach their answer within about two clicks of the homepage. Weak sites are written only for the first.

| Audience | The decision they're making | What actually convinces them |
|---|---|---|
| Prospective members | "Is this worth a chunk of my semester?" | Concrete description of what each team *does weekly*, honest recruitment process and dates, visible member outcomes |
| Companies, sponsors, partners | "Is this a serious counterparty for my brand and budget?" | Volume and regularity of published output, audience size, existing partners, a named contact and a real email |
| Recruiters and the job market | "Are these people any good?" | Signed work with technical depth, alumni destinations, portfolio methodology |
| The university and faculty | "Does associating with this reflect well on us?" | Governance, history, alignment with the academic mission, careful risk language |

A fifth, lower-stakes audience is peer clubs looking for collaborators — they are convinced by the same things partners are.

## The principles that make a site read as credible

**1. Proof over promise.** Replace claims with artifacts. "We produce rigorous research" is worth nothing; a PDF dated last month with two named authors and a stated methodology is worth everything. Every adjective on the site should have something clickable behind it.

**2. Cadence is the strongest signal available to a student club.** Institutions distrust student organisations for one reason: turnover. A monthly publication that has not missed an issue in three years is the single most persuasive thing a club can show, because it proves the club survives its own graduations. Make cadence *visible* — dates on everything, in reverse chronological order, so the rhythm is legible at a glance.

**3. Name humans.** Bylines on articles, headshots and roles on team pages, direct emails for department heads, links to professional profiles. Anonymous output reads as unserious and, worse, gives members no personal stake. Attribution is simultaneously a credibility device and a retention device.

**4. Show the organisation's structure.** Departments or divisions, each with a stated remit and stated deliverables, tell an outsider "this is an organisation, not a group chat". Structure also tells a prospective member exactly which door to knock on.

**5. Publish honest numbers.** If the club runs a portfolio, state the strategy, the benchmark, the risk profile and the actual return — including bad months. Selective disclosure is instantly recognisable to anyone in finance and destroys more trust than a losing quarter ever would. Include a plain statement that portfolios are simulated/educational and nothing on the site is investment advice.

**6. Be legible to outsiders.** A first-year with no finance background, and a recruiter with thirty seconds, should both understand what the club is and does within about fifteen seconds of landing. Jargon belongs inside the work, not in the positioning.

**7. Freshness is a contract.** The moment a site publicly advertises something stale, it does active damage — it says nobody has cared for a year. A small site that is current beats a large site that is decaying. Never build a page the club cannot commit to maintaining.

**8. Restraint reads as confidence.** Clean, quiet, consistent, few colours, generous spacing, one typographic voice, no stock-photo filler. Financial institutions look calm; clubs that want to be taken seriously by them should too. Visual noise reads as compensation.

## The canonical structure

This is the shape nearly every excellent club site converges on. The names vary; the *functions* do not. Use it as a starting sitemap and adapt.

| Section | Function it performs | Why the section exists at all |
|---|---|---|
| **Home** | Orientation and routing | One screen must answer *who, where, what*; the rest of the page routes each audience to their page |
| **About** | Legitimacy | Mission, founding, history, how the club is governed, scale. Answers "are you real and will you still exist next year" |
| **Departments / Teams** | Concreteness | Turns an abstract club into specific, joinable roles with specific outputs |
| **Work / Publications** | The centrepiece | The evidence. Everything else on the site points here |
| **Portfolio / Strategies** | Skin in the game | Optional but powerful: demonstrates method and discipline, not just commentary |
| **Events** | Reach and convening power | Shows the club can gather people and attract speakers — what sponsors are buying |
| **Partners** | Social proof and a sales page | Existing logos reassure the next partner; also states what a partner gets |
| **Team** | Faces and accountability | Current board, current members, past boards, alumni |
| **Join / Recruitment** | Conversion | Process, stages, criteria, dates, current status |
| **Contact** | Accessibility | A real address, a real email, active social links |

A club without publications yet should still ship this structure minus the sections it cannot fill — but see *Sequencing* below, because launching with empty core sections is worse than launching smaller.

Detailed content blueprints for each page are in `references/page-blueprints.md`. Read that file whenever the task involves drafting or auditing specific pages rather than the overall shape.

## The publication archive, in more detail

This is where reputation is actually manufactured, so it deserves disproportionate care.

- **Every item carries**: title, publication date, named authors, a one-or-two-sentence abstract, a type tag, and a way to read or download it.
- **The abstract matters more than people expect.** It lets a visitor judge relevance without opening a PDF, and it lets search engines and shared links carry substance.
- **Keep the internal structure of recurring publications constant** across issues. Predictable sections make a newsletter feel like a product rather than a school assignment, and make it far easier for successive cohorts to produce.
- **Order reverse-chronologically and let the archive go deep.** Depth is the point: fifty issues going back years is an asset no new club can fake.
- **Distinguish publication types** (recurring newsletter vs. one-off deep dive vs. valuation report vs. weekly market note) and let visitors filter. Different audiences want different types.
- **Never break the sequence silently.** If the club pauses a series, say so on the page in one plain line. An acknowledged pause reads as honest; an unexplained six-month gap reads as collapse.
- **State sourcing standards once**, somewhere near the archive — where data comes from, what review the work passes through before publishing. This is a cheap, high-credibility move that almost no student club makes.

## Positioning and voice

The site must answer four questions explicitly, in the club's own words, near the top of About:

1. **Who we are** — a student-run organisation at a named institution.
2. **Where we sit** — the faculty, the campus, the student population it draws from.
3. **What is distinctive about our angle** — the honest differentiator, which for many clubs is the background of their members or the kind of work they can do that others cannot.
4. **What we produce** — the concrete outputs, named.

On voice: write plainly, in the third person or a restrained first-person plural, in one language consistently. Avoid superlative self-description ("the leading source of...", "excellence-driven") — an unbacked superlative reads as insecurity to exactly the audience the club is trying to impress. Let numbers, dates and artifacts carry the boasting.

On differentiation: if a club's edge is technical (engineering, mathematics, computing backgrounds rather than management), the right way to express that is through *the nature of the work displayed* — methodology sections, models, code-adjacent projects, quantitative strategies with stated assumptions — not through a sentence asserting that the club is more technical. Show the work that only this club could produce.

## Visual and structural quality

Judged as a product manager, not a designer:

- **One template per content type.** Every publication card looks the same; every team card looks the same; every event page follows one shape. Inconsistency is the fastest way to look amateur.
- **A single restrained palette and one or two typefaces**, applied identically everywhere including on the PDFs the club publishes. The report and the website should obviously come from the same organisation.
- **Photography of the actual club** — cohort photos, event photos, the room — beats stock imagery every time. Where no real photo exists, use nothing.
- **Consistent naming.** If the site says "departments" in the menu, it should not say "divisions" on the page and "teams" in the footer.
- **Every navigation item leads somewhere distinct.** Menu entries that all resolve to the same page are a common and very visible failure.
- **Mobile matters more than the club expects**, because most campus discovery happens through a phone after seeing a social post.

## Maintenance: the part that decides whether the site works

Most club sites fail here, not at launch. Build the maintenance model into the plan from day one.

- **Assign ownership.** One named role — usually inside the communications/external-relations function — owns the site and is accountable for it. Not "the board".
- **Write the annual rollover checklist** and run it at every board transition: team page, board list, academic-year labels, recruitment dates and status, cohort photo, footer year, any "current" claims, any dated statistics.
- **Date or delete statistics.** "58 members" without a year is a liability; "58 members (2025/26)" is a fact.
- **Recruitment pages need a state, not just content.** Open / closing on a date / closed. A recruitment page advertising last year's deadline is one of the most damaging things a club site can display, because it is exactly the page prospective members land on.
- **Prefer fewer, living pages.** If a section cannot survive a change of cohort, do not build it.

## Sequencing for a young club

A new club should not attempt the full structure at once. Excellence at small scale beats a large hollow site.

- **V0 — launch (about five pages).** Home, About, Departments, Team, Join/Contact. Launch only when the club can commit to publishing its first piece of dated work within roughly a month; otherwise the site is a promise with nothing behind it.
- **V1 — the credibility engine.** Add the Publications archive with a fixed cadence, and an Events page as soon as there is a first event. This is the phase that converts a website into a track record.
- **V2 — the institutional layer.** Add Partners, Portfolio/Strategies with methodology and results, Alumni, past boards, a subscribe mechanism. These are the pages that speak to sponsors and recruiters, and they only work once V1 has run long enough to have depth.

## Common failure modes to check for

A fuller audit rubric is in `references/review-rubric.md`; read it when the task is to evaluate or critique an existing site. The recurring ones, seen across otherwise-strong club sites:

- Template placeholder copy left live on a real page
- Buttons and links with default labels, or links that go nowhere
- Recruitment pages advertising an expired deadline
- Stale footer copyright or academic year
- Numbered processes with a missing step
- The same paragraph repeated several times on one page
- Multiple menu entries resolving to the same anchor
- Undated statistics
- Unexplained gaps in a publication archive
- Photo galleries used as the primary evidence of work
- Mission-statement inflation with nothing behind it
- No named contact, only a generic form

## How to respond when using this skill

Give the user the *reasoning*, not just a sitemap. For each section recommended, say what job it does and for which audience — that is what lets them adapt it to a club whose departments and members do not match anyone else's.

Default to a structured answer: principles first, then structure, then page-level specifics, then the maintenance model. Use tables for the sitemap and for audience mapping. Keep prose tight. When the user's own club context is known, apply the framework to it rather than restating the framework.
