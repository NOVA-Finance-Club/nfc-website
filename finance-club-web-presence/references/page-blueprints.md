# Page Blueprints

Content blueprints for each page of a finance club website. Each blueprint states the page's job, the blocks it should contain in order, and the reasoning. Adapt names and blocks to the club's actual structure — the *functions* are what transfer, not the labels.

## Home

**Job:** orient any visitor in seconds, then route each of the four audiences to the page that answers their question. The homepage does not need to *contain* the answers — it needs to make finding them obvious.

**Blocks, in order:**

1. **Hero.** Club name, and one line stating what it is and where. Not a slogan — a definition. A visitor who reads only this line should be able to describe the club to someone else.
2. **A short positioning paragraph.** Two to four sentences: founded when, run by whom, what it exists to do. This is the sentence a journalist or a sponsor would quote.
3. **What we do.** Three to six tiles, each a real activity category with a one-line description and a link to the page that proves it. Categories, not adjectives.
4. **Latest work.** The three most recent publications, with dates and abstracts, pulled from the archive so this block ages automatically rather than manually. This is the single highest-value block on the homepage: it is live proof, and it never needs editing.
5. **Scale in numbers.** Members, departments, distinct academic backgrounds, alumni, subscribers, publications to date, event attendance. Four to six figures, each dated. Choose figures that are *true and unusual* rather than impressive-sounding — "12 distinct academic backgrounds" says more about a club than a round follower count.
6. **The team.** Cohort photograph plus a link to the full team page. A real photo of real people is disproportionately reassuring.
7. **Partners.** A logo row, if any exist. If none exist yet, omit the block entirely rather than showing an empty or padded one.
8. **A single primary call to action.** Usually join-us during recruitment season, subscribe-to-our-work otherwise. One CTA, changed seasonally, beats three competing ones.

**Common mistakes:** a hero that is a slogan with no information; a "what we do" section written in aspirational future tense; a latest-work block that is manually maintained and therefore six months old.

## About

**Job:** establish that the club is legitimate, durable and institutionally anchored. This is the page a faculty member, a sponsor's compliance team, or a cautious prospective member reads.

**Blocks, in order:**

1. **Mission** — what the club exists to achieve, in plain language, one short paragraph.
2. **Vision or goals** — two to four concrete objectives. Concrete means checkable: producing work of a given standard, connecting members to industry, developing skills beyond coursework.
3. **Who we are** — student-run, at which faculty, drawing from which populations, how many people.
4. **How we are organised** — a sentence on governance (board, elected or appointed, department heads) plus a pointer to the departments page. Governance detail is boring to students and important to institutions; keep it brief but present.
5. **History timeline** — founding year and the handful of moments that mattered: founding, formal recognition, opening membership more widely, first flagship event, first publication. A timeline is the cheapest possible proof of durability, and a young club with three entries still benefits from having one, because it signals intent to keep a record.
6. **Cross-links** to Departments, Team and Join.

**Note:** for a young club, honesty about age is stronger than vagueness. "Founded in 2025" plus a visible cadence of work reads better than an undated site that implies more history than exists.

## Departments / Teams

**Job:** convert the abstract club into specific, joinable roles with specific outputs. This page does more recruitment work than the recruitment page does.

**Structure:** one consistent block per department. Every block contains:

1. **Name and one-line remit.**
2. **What this department actually does**, in a short paragraph — the weekly and monthly reality, not the aspiration.
3. **Key responsibilities or sub-teams**, as a short list. Sub-teams are worth showing when a department is large: they demonstrate real internal organisation and give members a sense of progression.
4. **What it produces** — the concrete deliverables that appear elsewhere on the site. This is the link between structure and evidence.
5. **Who leads it** — head, photo, contact.
6. **Who it suits** — one line on the kind of member who thrives there. This is the block that converts an undecided reader.

**Reasoning:** an outsider judges organisational maturity by whether internal structure is legible. A department page that lists deliverables also creates internal accountability — the club has publicly stated what each team owes.

**Common mistakes:** describing departments only by topic ("Financial Markets") rather than by activity; writing the same generic paragraph for every department; repeating identical department text several times on the page; listing a department with no output and no lead.

## Work / Publications

**Job:** be the evidence. This is the most important page on the site.

**Structure:**

1. **A short framing block** at the top: what the club publishes, on what cadence, where the data comes from, and what review the work passes before publication. Three or four sentences. Almost no student club does this, and it is one of the highest-credibility-per-word blocks available.
2. **Type filters** — recurring newsletter, deep dives, valuation or equity reports, weekly market notes, journals. Different audiences want different things and filtering respects that.
3. **A reverse-chronological list** where every entry carries: cover thumbnail, title, date, named authors, one-to-two-sentence abstract, type tag, and a read/download action.
4. **Pagination or load-more** so the archive can go arbitrarily deep without slowing the page.

**Rules that matter:**

- Consistent internal section structure across issues of a recurring publication. It makes the output feel like a product and makes handover between cohorts far easier.
- Never break the sequence silently. One plain line — "this series is currently paused" — is the correct treatment for an interruption.
- Abstracts should describe the *specific* content of that issue, not the generic template. An archive where every abstract reads identically tells a visitor the issues are interchangeable.
- Individual publications benefit from having their own web page, not only a PDF, because a page can be linked, shared and previewed properly.

## Portfolio / Strategies

**Job:** demonstrate method and discipline. Optional, but it is the section that most sharply separates clubs that *discuss* finance from clubs that *do* it — and for a technically-minded membership it is often the strongest differentiator available.

**Per strategy or portfolio, show:**

1. **Name and risk profile** (conservative / balanced / growth, or equivalent).
2. **Mandate** — what the strategy is trying to achieve, in one paragraph, including a target and a time horizon.
3. **The benchmark, named explicitly**, with a sentence on why that benchmark was chosen. Choosing and justifying a benchmark is the clearest possible signal of seriousness.
4. **Methodology** — how positions are selected, how weights are set, rebalancing frequency, the asset universe. Depth here is welcome; this is where technical members should be allowed to show their reasoning.
5. **Performance metrics** — cumulative return, annualised return, volatility, Sharpe, max drawdown, and comparison against the benchmark, over selectable time windows.
6. **Holdings**, or at least top positions, with weights.
7. **A plain disclaimer**: simulated/paper portfolio, educational purpose, not investment advice.

**Rules:** report losses as visibly as gains; never launch this section with placeholder zeros and "loading" states that never resolve, because a permanently empty metrics table is worse than no metrics section at all.

## Events

**Job:** show convening power. This is what sponsors are actually buying, and what makes a club visible on campus.

**Structure:**

1. **Recurring flagship events get their own page each** — a competition, a summit, a trip, a workshop series. A flagship with several past editions is an institution; a one-off is an activity.
2. **Each flagship page contains:** what it is, who it is open to, the format and timeline, past editions with dates and outcomes, jury or speakers, sponsors, and photos of the real thing.
3. **Outcomes over descriptions.** Number of participants, number of teams, what winners received, who judged. Specific outcomes are what make a page persuasive to the next sponsor.
4. **A "more events" or archive page** for smaller talks, workshops and visits.
5. **Upcoming events must have a state.** Open for registration, closed, or past. An event page in permanent future tense for an event that already happened is a very common and very visible failure.

## Partners

**Job:** two things at once — reassure with existing partners, and sell to the next one.

**Blocks:**

1. **Current partner logos**, grouped by tier if tiers exist.
2. **What partnership involves** — the concrete formats available: sponsoring a competition, hosting a workshop, a company visit, recruiting access, brand presence at a flagship event.
3. **What the club offers in return, quantified** — audience size, reach across faculties, publication subscribers, event attendance, social following. Partners are making a media and recruitment decision and need numbers.
4. **Testimonials from existing partners**, attributed to a named person and role. A partner willing to be quoted is powerful third-party validation.
5. **A named contact and a direct email** for partnership enquiries. Not a generic form.

**Note:** a club with no partners yet should not fake this page. Instead, a short "work with us" page stating the formats on offer and the audience is honest and still useful.

## Team

**Job:** faces, accountability and internal motivation.

**Structure:**

1. **Board first**, then members grouped by department, in a single consistent card format.
2. **Each card:** photo, full name, role, professional profile link, and — for heads and board — a direct email.
3. **Cohort photo** with the academic year labelled.
4. **Past boards page**, once one exists. Showing predecessors demonstrates continuity and keeps alumni attached to the club.

**Reasoning:** photos and names do more than humanise. They give every member a personal reason to care about the quality of the site, and they let a recruiter connect published work to a real person.

**Rules:** consistent photo treatment across all cards — same crop, same background style, same framing. Mixed photo styles are the single most visible amateurism signal on a club site. Label the academic year so the page is obviously current, and roll it over at every transition.

## Alumni

**Job:** show where the experience leads. This is the page that converts ambitious prospective members and reassures recruiters.

**Blocks:**

1. **Alumni destinations** — the institutions and firms where former members ended up, as a logo grid or a list. This is the club's outcome data.
2. **A small number of alumni profiles** with a line each on what they did in the club and what they do now.
3. **How alumni stay involved** — mentoring, speaking, hosting visits — which signals a functioning network rather than a mailing list.

A young club has no alumni and should omit this page entirely rather than pad it. It becomes available in year two or three, and is worth planning for by keeping records from the start.

## Join / Recruitment

**Job:** convert interest into applications, and set expectations honestly.

**Blocks, in order:**

1. **Current status, stated first and unmissably.** Open until a date / opening in a given month / closed. This is the only page where the state matters more than the content.
2. **Who can apply** — which faculties, which years, which backgrounds. Be explicit and be generous; clubs consistently underestimate how much students self-exclude.
3. **Why join** — four to six concrete reasons. Skills, real projects, network, mentorship, community. Concrete beats inspirational: name the actual things a member will have done by the end of the year.
4. **The process, step by step, numbered and complete**, with dates and what each stage assesses. Applicants find opacity stressful, and a clear process signals an organisation that runs on rules rather than friendships.
5. **What is expected in return** — realistic time commitment. Stating this filters well and reduces attrition.
6. **A link to Departments** for anyone unsure where they fit, and a contact route for questions.

**The rollover rule:** this page must be updated at the start of every cycle. An expired deadline here is the most damaging staleness on the entire site, because this is the page that gets shared most.

## Contact and footer

**Job:** be reachable and look institutionally real.

**Contains:** a general email, department emails where relevant, physical address including the faculty and campus, active links to the social channels the club actually maintains, and — where the club is a formally constituted body — its legal name and registration.

**Footer rules:** the same footer everywhere, with the current year, the club's one-line positioning, and only the social links that are actually alive. A dead social link in the footer is a small thing that reads as neglect. Do not list a channel the club has not posted to in a year.
